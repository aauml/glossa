<Lede>
The United States Fifth Fleet calls Bahrain home. The fleet itself, Zulfiqar tells his interviewer, is 'presently in Islamabad,' its officers holding conversations about Bahrain joining a Saudi-Pakistani defense agreement, a pact that has also invited Iran. Take that at his valuation and it is extraordinary: the host of American naval power in the Gulf negotiating its way into a framework that courts Tehran. Zulfiqar takes it at his valuation, and he does not stop there. In his telling, the pact, Iran's uranium, and Washington's own sanctions are a single story, and the story ends with the petrodollar. Some of his numbers survive contact with the public record. The most dramatic one does not. What is harder to dismiss is the shape of the story itself: a region, he says, quietly rearranging itself without asking permission.
</Lede>

<Section number="01" title="The pact that <em>invites Iran</em>">

<Standfirst>Before the uranium and the fleet there is a piece of paper, and a guest list. Zulfiqar's argument starts with a defense agreement he says was concluded in Mecca.</Standfirst>

The conversation this reading follows belongs to a genre that thrives wherever American power is discussed from outside Washington: the autopsy performed in advance. Its central voice is Zulfiqar, joined by the analyst Parveen Sawhney, and neither pretends to neutrality. They assume that U.S. policy in the region is failing and that regional powers are already acting on the failure, and everything they say is offered in that spirit. This publication does not adopt their premise; it does hold that an argument traveling widely is worth laying out cleanly and checking carefully.

Zulfiqar's foundation is a piece of paper. In his account, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan have concluded a defense agreement at Mecca, and the circle is widening: Bahrain is discussing accession, and Iran, the state Gulf security architecture was long built to contain, has been invited in. If true, it inverts the region's geometry; for half a century its alignments ran through Washington, and here is an alignment that does not. He is aware of how it sounds, and he says it anyway.

<ContextBox label="What is the Mecca Agreement?">
The interview treats the agreement as settled fact and gives few particulars. As Zulfiqar describes it, it is a defense arrangement anchored by Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, with Bahrain weighing entry and Iran extended an invitation. He cites no text, no signing date, and no list of signatories, and the outside documents this publication consulted for verification do not cover it. The agreement therefore enters this reading the way it enters the interview: as a premise, offered by a speaker who finds it self-evident. Premises can be tested.
</ContextBox>

Why would such a pact exist now? Because the guarantor lost credibility, Zulfiqar answers: the United States can still punish, in his view, but it can no longer reassure, so the region is writing its own insurance policy, and the invitation to Tehran is the clause that would once have been unthinkable. A premise that sweeping needs corroboration, and he believes he has it; he reaches first for a number.

</Section>

<Section number="02" title="Ninety, or <em>sixty</em>">

<Standfirst>Zulfiqar's most alarming claim is a single number for Iran's uranium. The inspectors who count that uranium publish a different one.</Standfirst>

Five minutes in, Zulfiqar delivers the interview's most alarming sentence: Iran, he says, has increased its uranium enrichment to ninety percent. He presents it not as a leak but as a message, Tehran's calculated answer to years of sanctions, escalation meant to be seen. In his reading, a cornered state is showing Washington exactly how close it can get.

The people whose job is to count Iran's uranium publish a different number. The IAEA's quarterly report, as parsed by the Institute for Science and International Security, recorded 164.7 kilograms of uranium enriched to sixty percent as of mid-August 2024; by November the Arms Control Association's tally read 182 kilograms at sixty percent, alongside 840 at twenty and about 2,600 at five. Ninety percent, the level inspectors call weapons-grade, appears in no ledger this publication could consult. The most alarming claim in the interview is, on the available record, wrong.

<ContextBox label="What does the enrichment percentage measure?">
The percentage measures the share of U-235, the isotope that sustains a chain reaction, in the uranium produced. Power reactors run on low single digits; what inspectors call weapons-grade is around ninety percent. Material at sixty percent is far beyond anything a power program needs, which is why inspectors track it so closely. The climb from sixty to ninety is, in the physics of centrifuges, far shorter than the climb from five to sixty. That is why the real number alarms inspectors without any help from Zulfiqar's.
</ContextBox>

The machinery behind the percentage is real and large. The IAEA counts about 10,600 advanced centrifuges installed at Natanz and Fordow, with total enrichment capacity near 48,800 separative work units a year, and estimates Fordow alone could yield thirty-seven kilograms of sixty-percent material a month once planned expansions finish. U.S. intelligence, for its part, assesses that Iran is not currently building a weapon, only improving its position to build one if it chooses, while Kamal Kharrazi, an adviser to the Supreme Leader, has said Iran would modify its nuclear doctrine "if an existential threat arises." None of this is ninety percent; all of it runs in the direction Zulfiqar describes.

One more entry in the ledger cuts his way. Iran has withdrawn the designations of experienced inspectors and left surveillance equipment dark, and the IAEA says it "has lost continuity of knowledge" over centrifuge production and key materials; the head of Iran's atomic agency, Mohammad Eslami, answers that "Iran's position on the matter is unchanged and this position will remain as it is." The world is arguing about a stockpile it can no longer fully watch, and fog is where numbers like ninety percent breed. From uranium, the conversation turns, as Gulf arguments eventually do, to ships.

</Section>

<Section number="03" title="A fleet <em>between ports</em>">

<Standfirst>Bahrain hosts the U.S. Navy's Gulf command, and in Zulfiqar's account it is also weighing a pact built by others. He says the fleet has already felt the room tilt.</Standfirst>

The fleet claim arrives three minutes and forty-five seconds in, almost casually, and paraphrase would flatten what is interesting in it. Here it is as he said it:

<QABlock speaker="Zulfiqar">
The Fifth Fleet is presently in Islamabad, having conversations with the second person in command about Bahrain joining the Saudi Arabia–Pakistan defense agreement.
</QABlock>

Read it twice. Ships cannot reach Islamabad, which sits far from any sea, so the travelers must be officers: the fleet's command, in his account, is doing political work in Pakistan's capital while Bahrain's leadership discusses joining the Saudi-Pakistani pact. No document consulted for this reading confirms any relocation of the fleet's headquarters, and the interview supplies none. But Zulfiqar's underlying point is not nautical: a headquarters abroad is a tenancy, and tenants watch their landlords.

<ContextBox label="What is the Fifth Fleet?">
The Fifth Fleet is the United States Navy's command for the Persian Gulf and the waters around the Arabian Peninsula, the corridor through which a large share of the world's traded oil moves. Its headquarters sits in Bahrain, an island kingdom whose security the American presence has underwritten for decades. That address gives Zulfiqar's story its voltage: if Bahrain were to join a pact built by Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, one that also opens a door to Iran, the fleet's host would be hedging against its guarantor.
</ContextBox>

Nothing in his account requires a single hull to move. The claim is about alignment: the state hosting America's Gulf fleet weighing a structure America did not design, in company with a state America sanctions. Basing rights are consent renewed year by year, and his suggestion is that the consent is being quietly renegotiated. What changed the hosts, in his telling, was not a battle but a ledger.

</Section>

<Section number="04" title="The bill for <em>maximum pressure</em>">

<Standfirst>His economics carry his politics: sanctions taught the region to live without the dollar, he argues, and the teacher is running out of credit.</Standfirst>

The economics arrive with a cast. Donald Trump is named as the architect of the maximum pressure campaign against Iran, and Scott Bessent as an advocate of economic pressure as a geopolitical tool against it; JD Vance's stated perspectives on U.S. foreign policy are cited along the way. In Zulfiqar's reading these men built a weapon that fires in both directions. Sanction a country and you show it the choke point; sanction enough countries and you have advertised the detour.

The detour, he says, is already in use: regional states settling more of their trade in local currencies, shaving the world's structural need for dollars. This is where the argument reaches the word the whole interview has been circling.

<ContextBox label="What is the petrodollar?">
It is not a treaty but a habit with a treaty's force. Since the 1970s the world's oil has been priced and settled overwhelmingly in dollars, and exporting states have recycled their earnings into American assets. The habit hands Washington two privileges: a standing global demand for its currency, which keeps its borrowing cheap, and a clearing system only it ultimately controls, which makes its sanctions bite. If oil and trade migrate to other currencies, both privileges shrink at once. That migration, in Zulfiqar's account, is now underway.
</ContextBox>

Twenty-eight minutes in, he supports the point with two round numbers: a national debt, he says, above forty trillion dollars, and an annual defense budget above one trillion. He cites no source for either, and the documents consulted for this reading address neither, so both travel here as his figures, not this publication's. The precision matters less to him than the shape: a guarantor whose obligations, in his view, have outgrown the credibility that once made its protection worth hosting.

Put the economics beside the pact and the fleet and the interview's thesis stands complete: security, basing, and settlement all sliding off the American system at once, each slide confirming the others. If the old order is being vacated, something has to be proposed for the space. Sawhney gives the proposal a name.

</Section>

<Section number="05" title="What <em>collective security</em> would look like">

<Standfirst>Sawhney states the endgame in one breath: American forces out, a regional framework in. The public record hints at how Tehran wants to get there.</Standfirst>

The name arrives about seven and a half minutes in, and it comes from Sawhney. It is the interview's thesis in a single breath.

<PullQuote>Iran's objective is to get the US forces out of West Asia, and replace it with a regional framework based on collective security.</PullQuote>

Collective security is an old phrase with an idealist pedigree: a neighborhood where everyone's safety is guaranteed by everyone, and no outside landlord is required. Coming from a discussion of Iran, it carries a pointed irony, since Tehran's file at the IAEA has rarely been thicker. The agency's Board of Governors censured Iran in June 2024 and again in November, and the Security Council closed the year reviewing the nuclear file under resolution 2231, with an exchange of direct strikes between Iran and Israel in the background. The state the interview casts as a future guarantor of the region is, in the formal record, the region's principal proliferation concern.

Tehran, for its part, speaks in two voices at once. President Masoud Pezeshkian says that "if JCPOA commitments are implemented fully and in good faith, dialogue on other issues can follow," and Iran has floated a halt to sixty-percent production, an offer France, Germany, and Britain dismissed as insufficient, demanding the existing stockpile be disposed of instead. A state engineering a rupture does not usually bother floating partial concessions; a state hedging does. The record reads less like Zulfiqar's changing of the guard than like a region keeping two doors open at once.

The way to read what comes next is by dials, not verdicts. The first dial is the pact: whether the Mecca agreement acquires a text, a date, and signatories, or remains a premise carried by interviews like this one. The second is the stockpile: whether the floated halt on sixty-percent production happens while Fordow's expansions wait. The third is the cameras: whether the IAEA regains the continuity of knowledge it says it has lost, because a stockpile no one watches is a rumor with mass.

Zulfiqar's ninety percent was wrong. The dark that made it sound plausible is still on.

</Section>
