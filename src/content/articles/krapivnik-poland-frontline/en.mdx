<Lede>
Stanislav Krapivnik was, by his own account, born in Donbas, commissioned as an officer in the United States Army, and is now fifteen years into a life in Russia. He is a man who changed sides, and he reads the map accordingly. His subject, in a long interview with the professor Glenn Diesen, is Poland: the spending, the shopping lists, the training grounds, the fortified border with Belarus. His claim is that none of it amounts to Polish policy in the way Warsaw uses the term. Poland, he says, is being prepared as the frontline state of a direct war between NATO and Russia — the country meant to fight first so others do not have to. It is a reading with a vested interest, and it deserves the suspicion that asks for. It also points at facts anyone can check: the fastest military build-up in Europe, on the alliance's most exposed flank, cheered on by its allies. What the build-up is for is where his argument begins.
</Lede>

<Section number="01" title="An American uniform, <em>a Russian address</em>">

<Standfirst>Before the argument, the man: a life that began in Donbas, passed through the U.S. Army, and settled in Russia.</Standfirst>

Stanislav Krapivnik <span class="said">is originally from Donbas</span>, the industrial east of Ukraine, and <span class="said">served as an officer in the United States Army</span>. <span class="said">Fifteen years ago he left America for Russia</span>, and he now comments from there on the alliance he once served. The biography is not incidental to the argument. It is the argument's ground floor: a man formed on both sides of the line he now describes.

<ContextBox label="What is Donbas?">
The Donbas, short for Donets Basin, is the industrial region of eastern Ukraine made up of the Donetsk and Luhansk oblasts. Coal and steel made it a heartland of Soviet heavy industry, and Russian remains the dominant language in its cities. In 2014, after Russia annexed Crimea, Moscow-backed formations seized parts of both oblasts and proclaimed 'people's republics.' The war that followed killed some fourteen thousand people over eight years. Russia recognized the republics days before its full-scale invasion of February 2022, and now claims to have annexed both regions outright.
</ContextBox>

The man asking the questions is Glenn Diesen, a professor who hosts long conversations about geopolitics, and he lets his guest run. What the guest runs with is a premise, and it deserves stating once: the West is not managing a confrontation with Russia but manufacturing one, and Poland is the centerpiece. The premise is contestable. Much of what gets stacked on it is checkable. He starts with the steel.

</Section>

<Section number="02" title="The <em>arsenal</em> on the Vistula">

<Standfirst>Poland is buying weapons faster than any other country in Europe. The argument begins with what those weapons are for.</Standfirst>

Start with what is not in dispute. Poland now devotes more than four percent of GDP to defense, the largest share in NATO, ahead of the United States. The order books run to hundreds of tanks and howitzers, rocket artillery by the hundred, and the country's first stealth fighters. The defense ministry speaks openly of building the strongest land army on NATO's European flank.

<ContextBox label="What is Poland's military modernization program?">
Poland's shopping list mixes suppliers in a way no other European country attempts. From South Korea it has ordered K2 tanks and K9 howitzers, with part of the production licensed to Polish factories. From the United States come Abrams tanks, HIMARS rocket launchers, Patriot air-defense batteries, and F-35 stealth fighters. The purchases are financed in part with Korean loans and are meant to replace the Soviet-era equipment Poland sent to Ukraine. Warsaw's declared goal is a land force strong enough to deter Russia on Polish soil before allies could arrive.
</ContextBox>

Krapivnik reads the list the other way. A country arming to hold its own ground buys air defense, mines, and drones. Poland is buying <span class="attr">heavy armor and long-range strike, the equipment of an army meant to move forward</span>. The tell, for him, is the wiring: <span class="attr">Polish brigades plugged into American command systems, American ammunition, American plans</span>. The conclusion he draws is that <span class="attr">the build-up is an assignment, not a choice</span>, an arsenal paid for in Warsaw but sized for someone else's war.

An assignment implies a theater. In his geography, the theater is already at war, and it begins at Poland's eastern border.

</Section>

<Section number="03" title="The <em>staging ground</em>">

<Standfirst>Since 2022 Poland has served as the West's rear base for Ukraine. Krapivnik's reading is that it is a rehearsal, not a coincidence.</Standfirst>

The visible record is straightforward. Since February 2022, Poland has been the main conduit of Western military aid to Ukraine: weapons land at Rzeszów, an hour from the border, and transship east. Polish soil hosts the European Union's training mission for Ukrainian soldiers, and Polish workshops repair equipment for the front. Warsaw presents all of it as solidarity, and as prudence.

Where Warsaw sees solidarity, Krapivnik sees apprenticeship. <span class="attr">The hub is the rehearsal</span>: the same airfields, railheads, and depots a Polish army would need in a direct war. <span class="attr">Ukraine, in this reading, demonstrated the model</span>, a partner the alliance arms, trains, and supplies but does not fight for. <span class="attr">Poland is being groomed as the model's next host</span> — larger, treaty-bound, and ready the day Ukraine can no longer serve.

A rehearsal implies an opening night. The hardest question is what happens if the hub itself is struck, and which words would then be read aloud in Brussels.

</Section>

<Section number="04" title="The <em>corridor</em>, and the clause">

<Standfirst>A direct war would need a flashpoint and a justification. Geography supplies the first. NATO's founding treaty supplies the second.</Standfirst>

Debris from Russia's strikes on Ukraine has landed on NATO territory more than once, and each time the alliance has absorbed it. Krapivnik reads the pattern as conditioning, not restraint. <span class="attr">Every incident that passes without war lowers the public threshold for the next one</span>, and the talk around Article 5 grows more practiced with each rehearsal. <span class="attr">Western capitals, in his account, are normalizing the idea of direct war with Russia</span>, teaching their publics to expect the clash before it has a name.

<ContextBox label="What is Article 5?">
Article 5 is the collective-defense clause of NATO's founding treaty of 1949: an armed attack on one member counts as an attack on all. It does not trigger war automatically. Each ally takes the action it judges necessary. The clause has been invoked once, after the attacks of September 11, 2001. In November 2022 a missile that killed two people in the Polish village of Przewodów proved to be Ukrainian air defense, and the alliance declined to call it an attack. Poland is covered by the clause. Ukraine, not a member, is not.
</ContextBox>

<ContextBox label="What is the Suwałki Gap?">
The Suwałki Gap is the strip of land along the Polish-Lithuanian border, about sixty-five kilometres across at its narrowest, separating Kaliningrad, a heavily armed Russian exclave, from Belarus, Moscow's closest ally. It is the only land corridor between the Baltic states and the rest of NATO. Since 2014 it has anchored Western war planning: cut the corridor and Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania cannot be reinforced by land. Russia keeps missiles in Kaliningrad and troops in Belarus, which by Moscow's own account now also hosts Russian nuclear weapons. That is why this short stretch of farmland keeps showing up in war games.
</ContextBox>

He also runs the map in reverse. The corridor NATO fears losing is, seen from Moscow, an exposure of its own: Kaliningrad surrounded by alliance territory, Belarus pressed against a Poland that arms fast. <span class="attr">Russian planners, he says, read the build-up as preparation to take Kaliningrad or strike Belarus</span>, and they plan accordingly.

Each side's precaution becomes the other's evidence, and the space between deterrence and provocation closes. It is the oldest dynamic in European security, and it now runs hottest through Poland. The last variable is the Polish public, the arrangement's quietest assumption.

</Section>

<Section number="05" title="The politics of the <em>tripwire</em>">

<Standfirst>Rearmament is the rare thing Polish politics agrees on. The question is what, and whom, the consensus serves.</Standfirst>

The consensus is real. The party that governed Poland until 2023 began the buying spree. The coalition that replaced it, hostile to the old government on nearly everything else, kept the contracts and pressed on.

The reasons are sincere. Poland was partitioned by its neighbors in the eighteenth century, invaded from both directions in 1939, and held under Soviet domination for four decades. In Warsaw, Russia is not a theory. It is the recurring chapter, and doubt about deterrence reads as naïveté or worse.

Krapivnik hears the sincerity and discounts it. <span class="attr">Poland's elites, he argues, compete for Washington's favor</span>, and the competition has made dependence feel like sovereignty. <span class="attr">The public's genuine fear of Russia is being harvested</span>, in his account, to fix Poland in a role that serves American planning more than Polish survival. The costs, he suggests, are already visible: <span class="attr">the debt behind the Korean contracts, the recruiting targets the demography cannot meet</span>, and a border more militarized with every season.

What could falsify his reading is worth naming. If the build-up crests and the arsenal turns to pure territorial defense, the frontline thesis weakens. If the orders keep skewing to long-range strike and the exercises keep rehearsing a march east, it strengthens.

Watch, too, for the first serious Polish voice to ask whether the country is being armed for its own war or for someone else's. No one near power is asking it yet. That silence is either prudence or the point.

</Section>
