<Lede>
Kaminski (2023) asks what changes when law calls AI harms “risks.” This reading sets out her argument and her reservation. Then it shows why a 2025 federal memorandum moves part of the contrast between binding European rules and a voluntary U.S. framework inside the U.S. government. ([Kaminski 2023](https://www.bu.edu/bulawreview/files/2023/11/KAMINSKI.pdf))
</Lede>

<Orientation lang="en">
The assigned reading is the introduction of [Kaminski (2023)](https://www.bu.edu/bulawreview/files/2023/11/KAMINSKI.pdf). Stop when you can state her argument, her reservation and one verifiable quotation. The rest is context, not assignment: the Tempe case, the EU Regulation, the framework from NIST (the U.S. standards agency) and the memorandum from OMB (the White House budget office). Kaminski wrote before that memorandum. And she analyzes a draft EU act, not its final text.
</Orientation>

<Section number="1" title="The claim in the assigned reading">

Kaminski starts from a convergence. Lawmakers in the United States and the European Union both turned to risk regulation, meaning rules that assess and reduce harms, to govern AI. She calls this neither inevitable nor neutral. Framing harms as risks is a value-laden choice. ([Kaminski](https://www.bu.edu/bulawreview/files/2023/11/KAMINSKI.pdf))

Her reservation is the one to save. The introduction (p. 1352) says risk regulation “typically assumes a technology will be adopted despite its harms.” The abstract (p. 1347) is fuller. Risk regulation tends to fix problems with the technology so it may be used, “rather than contemplating that it might sometimes not be appropriate to use it at all.” ([Kaminski](https://www.bu.edu/bulawreview/files/2023/11/KAMINSKI.pdf))

Other costs follow. Risk regulation can obscure hard-to-quantify harms and marginalized people. It can cloak policy choices as technical ones. Yet Kaminski allows qualitative risk assessment too. Nor does preserving liability guarantee compensation: tort law may miss many AI harms. Her framework helps identify the type of risk regulation a law uses, including its limits. ([Kaminski](https://www.bu.edu/bulawreview/files/2023/11/KAMINSKI.pdf))

</Section>

<Section number="2" title="An anchor: Tempe, 2018">

This case comes from Part I of Kaminski's article, beyond the assigned introduction. In March 2018, a self-driving Uber in Tempe, Arizona, failed to detect a pedestrian. It alerted its backup driver only 0.2 seconds before impact. The car killed Elaine Herzberg, 49. Uber had cut safety operators from two per car to one. It had no policy for policing phone use. ([Kaminski](https://www.bu.edu/bulawreview/files/2023/11/KAMINSKI.pdf))

Imagined illustration, not Kaminski's: a regulator might require testing, redesigned shifts and monitoring. Kaminski does report that researchers, herself included, value ex ante regulation, meaning rules applied before use. It can prevent harms rather than merely compensate for them. ([Kaminski](https://www.bu.edu/bulawreview/files/2023/11/KAMINSKI.pdf))

What remains open is the deployment question. This framing is a reading aid, not Kaminski's wording or an exhaustive account. Question one: how do we make this system safer? Question two: should it operate on public roads at all? Risk regulation typically assumes adoption, although precautionary bans can prevent use. Prompt: which question does the governing document of a system you study answer? ([Kaminski](https://www.bu.edu/bulawreview/files/2023/11/KAMINSKI.pdf))

</Section>

<Section number="3" title="The voluntary side, and Kaminski on the draft EU act">

Kaminski places the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) at the soft end. In her words it is straightforwardly self-regulation: voluntary guidance, not top-down law. NIST itself says the Framework is intended to be voluntary, rights-preserving, non-sector-specific and use-case agnostic. ([Kaminski](https://www.bu.edu/bulawreview/files/2023/11/KAMINSKI.pdf); [NIST](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.100-1))

Voluntary does not mean silent on stopping. The Core has four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage. Managing might mean deploying a system as is, deploying it with more testing and controls, or decommissioning it. Non-deployment is an option, though the Framework itself does not compel that choice. ([Kaminski](https://www.bu.edu/bulawreview/files/2023/11/KAMINSKI.pdf))

On the European side, Kaminski analyzes the 2021 draft. She describes product-safety scaffolding: bans, conformity assessments (checks that a system meets requirements) and lifecycle risk management. For high-risk systems mainly affecting fundamental rights, it relied on self-assessment. She reports it contained no individual rights. These are claims about the draft. She also warns that a legal tool with the same name is not necessarily the same tool. ([Kaminski](https://www.bu.edu/bulawreview/files/2023/11/KAMINSKI.pdf))

</Section>

<Section number="4" title="The final EU Regulation: what binds, and whom">

The supplied EU text is the official Spanish version of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the AI Act. English descriptions here are paraphrases of that version. Recital 26 calls for proportionate binding rules based on risk. Recital 27 calls the 2019 ethics principles non-binding. This distinction concerns the legal character of the instruments; it does not establish compliance in practice. ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj?locale=es))

Scope comes before comparison. Article 2 connects coverage to the EU. It covers providers placing systems on its market or putting them into service there, wherever established; EU deployers; and third-country providers and deployers whose outputs are used in the EU. Other covered actors include importers, distributors, certain product manufacturers, authorized representatives and affected people located in the EU. Exclusive military, defence and national-security purposes are excluded. The research exclusions have separate conditions. ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj?locale=es))

Article 2(6) excludes systems or models specifically developed and put into service solely for scientific research and development. Using an otherwise covered system for research does not itself exempt it. Article 2(8) separately excludes research, testing and development activities before market introduction or entry into service, subject to applicable EU law. Real-world testing is not covered by that activity exclusion. ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj?locale=es))

Article 2(4) excludes qualifying third-country public authorities and international organizations using AI under international agreements or cooperation for law enforcement and judicial cooperation with the EU or member states. Sufficient guarantees for fundamental rights and freedoms are required. Article 2(10) excludes deployer obligations for natural persons using AI purely personally and non-professionally. Article 2(12) excludes systems released under free/open-source licences, unless marketed or put into service as high-risk systems or systems covered by Articles 5 or 50. ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj?locale=es))

Article 6 has two high-risk routes. One combines an Annex I product or its safety component with a required third-party conformity assessment. The other covers the uses listed in Annex III. Under Article 6(3), an Annex III use may be excluded if it poses no significant health, safety or fundamental-rights risk, including by not materially influencing decisions. At least one listed limited-task condition must also apply. Profiling people overrides this exception. Providers claiming the exception must document their assessment and register the system. This guide does not classify a particular system. ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj?locale=es))

Article 2(2) further limits the product route for Annex I, Section B products. Only Article 6(1), Articles 102–109 and Article 112 apply. Article 57 applies only insofar as high-risk requirements have entered the listed sectoral legislation. Thus, a high-risk classification does not make Articles 11–14 universally applicable. These boundaries qualify the comparison. ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj?locale=es))

Within their applicable scope, Articles 11–14 specify requirements. Article 11 requires technical documentation before placing a system on the market or putting it into service, kept updated. Small and medium-sized firms may use a simplified form. Article 12 requires systems to technically enable automatic event logging throughout their lifecycle, supporting traceability. ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj?locale=es))

Article 13 requires transparency enabling deployers to interpret and correctly use outputs, with instructions for use. Article 14 requires effective human oversight, proportionate to risk, autonomy and context. Paragraph 4 provides for not using a system, overriding outputs or stopping it, as appropriate and proportionate. ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj?locale=es))

Article 14(5) addresses remote biometric identification systems under Annex III, point 1(a), where their use is legally permitted. This category excludes biometric verification used solely to confirm a person's claimed identity. The deployer cannot act or decide on an identification until at least two people have each separately verified and confirmed it. They must have the necessary competence, training and authority. ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj?locale=es))

The exception concerns law enforcement, migration, border control and asylum. It applies where Union or national law considers the separate verification by at least two people disproportionate. These conditions matter: the rule is not simply a recommendation to involve a second person. ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj?locale=es))

Article 16 places compliance obligations on providers of high-risk AI systems. Articles 13 and 14 connect providers and deployers through transparency and oversight. Those two provisions do not themselves establish an individual right to explanation or appeal. That observation does not establish the absence of such rights elsewhere in the final Regulation. ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj?locale=es))

The supplied 2024 text sets general application at August 2, 2026. Article 113 delays Article 6(1) and its corresponding obligations until August 2, 2027; some other provisions apply earlier. Article 111 treats ordinary high-risk systems already marketed or in service before August 2, 2026 differently. Their operators fall under the Regulation only if significant design changes occur from that date, without prejudice to Article 5's prohibitions. ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj?locale=es))

Separate legacy deadlines remain. Providers and deployers of high-risk systems intended for public authorities must take the necessary compliance measures by August 2, 2030. AI components of Annex X large-scale IT systems marketed or in service before August 2, 2027 must comply by December 31, 2030, without prejudice to Article 5. These are the supplied text's dates. This guide does not assess later amendments or establish current applicability to a particular system. ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj?locale=es))

Reading aid, not an equivalence claim. The selected EU provisions emphasize system documentation and design, linked to deployment practices. The RMF organizes risk management. Both involve ongoing organizational activity. The contrast is one of legal force and emphasis, not an opposition between design and organizational practice. ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj?locale=es)) ([Kaminski](https://www.bu.edu/bulawreview/files/2023/11/KAMINSKI.pdf))

</Section>

<Section number="5" title="Why the OMB memorandum moves the contrast inside the government">

M-25-21 is a memorandum from OMB to federal agencies. Penti reports it was issued on April 3, 2025, with its procurement companion M-25-22. In her account, the pair replace Biden-era directives of March 28 and September 24, 2024. They do not directly restrict private industry, though contracts may carry requirements to vendors. The memorandum itself governs agencies' own use of AI and creates no rights or obligations for the public. ([Penti](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2025/04/white-house-issues-guidance-on-use-and-procurement-of-artificial-intelligence-technology); [OMB](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/M-25-21.pdf))

Coverage follows the statutory agency definition in 44 U.S.C. § 3502(1), including independent regulatory agencies, rather than every federal body. It reaches new and existing AI developed, used or acquired by or on behalf of covered agencies. Requirements attach to AI functionality, not the entire incorporating information system. The AI-system definition excludes common commercial products with embedded AI, such as word processors. Some requirements apply only to the agencies identified by the Chief Financial Officers Act. Other applicable laws and OMB policies continue to govern. ([OMB](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/M-25-21.pdf))

High-impact AI is AI whose output is a principal basis for decisions or actions with legal, material, binding or significant effect. Six domains qualify: civil rights, liberties or privacy; access to education, housing, insurance, credit, employment or other programs; access to critical government resources or services; health and safety; critical infrastructure or public safety; and strategic assets or resources, including high-value property and sensitive or classified federal information. A use can qualify with or without human oversight. ([OMB](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/M-25-21.pdf))

Section 6 presumes some categories high-impact. Concluding otherwise requires written documentation to the agency's Chief AI Officer (CAIO). ([OMB](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/M-25-21.pdf))

For such uses, agencies must test before deployment and complete an impact assessment. Section 4(b)(ii)(C) includes potential impacts of using and not using AI. That addresses the deployment question on paper; it does not establish actual decisions. Assessments receive periodic updates and review by someone within the agency who did not develop the system. Risk acceptance requires a signature. Further practices include monitoring, training, human oversight and, when appropriate, timely human review and an opportunity to appeal negative impacts. Practices should be proportionate to anticipated risk. ([OMB](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/M-25-21.pdf))

The memorandum also directs agencies to accelerate effective, mission-enabling AI adoption and remove unnecessary bureaucratic barriers. This affirmative policy sits alongside the safeguards. ([OMB](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/M-25-21.pdf))

Section 4(a)(i) gives agencies 365 days from issuance to document implementation of the high-impact minimum practices. They must be ready to report to OMB through periodic reviews, the annual use-case inventory or on request. Separately, Section 4(b) requires pre-deployment testing and an impact assessment; Section 4(a) covers cessation, waivers and pilot exemptions. Later policy changes have not been checked here. ([OMB](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/M-25-21.pdf))

High-impact AI functionality that does not comply with the minimum practices must be safely discontinued until compliance is achieved, subject to applicable waivers or pilot exemptions. Separately, the memorandum requires cessation where proper mitigation is impossible. The CAIO, coordinating with relevant officials, may waive requirements through a written determination based on a system- and context-specific risk assessment. It must find that compliance would increase overall risks to safety or rights, or unacceptably impede critical operations. ([OMB](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/M-25-21.pdf))

The waiver authority cannot be delegated. Waivers are tracked centrally, recertified annually and reassessed after significant changes in conditions or context. OMB receives notice within 30 days of granting or revoking one. Agencies must publish a summary and justification of each determination and waiver, to the extent consistent with law and governmentwide policy, or publicly indicate that none are active. ([OMB](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/M-25-21.pdf))

Pilot exemptions require limited scale and duration, CAIO certification and central tracking. Minimum practices still apply where practicable. Where possible, participants must be able to opt in and out, with sufficient notice for an informed decision. ([OMB](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/M-25-21.pdf))

Sections 4(a) and 4(b) do not apply to Intelligence Community elements. AI used as a component of a National Security System is outside the memorandum. Basic or applied research uses are also excluded, except research developing particular AI applications for agency use. ([OMB](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/M-25-21.pdf))

Section 1(b) also excludes regulation of non-agency AI. It excludes assessments of particular AI applications conducted because their provider is an actual or potential target of regulatory enforcement, law enforcement or national-security action. Footnote 7 distinguishes using AI to carry out enforcement: that use is not excluded on this basis. Developing testing metrics, methods and standards for general public or governmentwide use is excluded too. ([OMB](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/M-25-21.pdf))

On NIST, Penti reports that Biden-era agencies were encouraged to use the RMF. She says the new guidelines diverge from NIST-related provisions of M-24-10 and M-24-18. Agencies are encouraged to develop their own practices. Contractors may face agency-specific requirements without NIST's harmonizing effect. Some RMF principles remain in the baseline. The earlier memoranda are not among the supplied sources. ([Penti](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2025/04/white-house-issues-guidance-on-use-and-procurement-of-artificial-intelligence-technology))

Commentary, not source. Kaminski contrasted a voluntary framework with a proposed European model intended to impose binding requirements. The enacted instrument discussed here is Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, with its own scope and application timetable. M-25-21 puts a version of that asymmetry inside federal practice. Under that memorandum, mandatory agency-directed practices sit beside a voluntary framework, and on Penti's reading the link between them loosened. ([OMB](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/M-25-21.pdf))

The triggers differ too. The EU Regulation and the memorandum use different categories. OMB's high-impact status turns on an output's effect. They are not equivalent. This is agency policy, not an enforceable individual right. A requirement on paper does not prove compliance. ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj?locale=es); [OMB](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/M-25-21.pdf))

Section 7 limits the memorandum's risk concept to risks from using AI that affect decision or action outcomes. It excludes some associated risks, such as privacy, security and confidentiality risks in training or input data. This narrower definition does not remove data-protection duties under other applicable laws and policies. ([OMB](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/M-25-21.pdf))

</Section>

<Section number="6" title="What to save, and what stays open">

Save three items. The argument: treating AI harms as risks is a value-laden choice. The reservation: risk regulation typically assumes adoption. The citation: the introduction's sentence quoted above. ([Kaminski](https://www.bu.edu/bulawreview/files/2023/11/KAMINSKI.pdf))

<ContextBox label="What this reading does not establish">
It does not show that Kaminski's draft-based claims, such as the absence of individual rights, describe the final EU text. It does not confirm applicability beyond the supplied text. It does not show how agencies apply M-25-21, how often they waive requirements or whether they comply. The historical account of M-24-10 and M-24-18 relies on Penti; their originals have not been independently verified here. Penti offers a law-firm reading, not the memorandum. Nothing here implies other comparisons are absent.
</ContextBox>

Coda. A binding text can require that a system be stoppable. A voluntary one can contemplate stopping it too. The difference lies in who can demand the stop. Kaminski's question survives both: who decides whether to deploy?

</Section>
