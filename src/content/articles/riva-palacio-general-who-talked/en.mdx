<Lede>
Andrés López Beltrán, son of Mexico's former president, <span class="said">lost his US visa five months before he said anything about it</span>. When he finally spoke, on August 13, it was in a public letter, and by then the letter was the least of the family's problems. A Mexican general, Gerardo Mérida, <span class="attr">is cooperating with the US Department of Justice</span>. What he handed over <span class="attr">links the younger López Beltrán to Los Chapitos, a faction of the Sinaloa Cartel</span>. The family <span class="said">weighed asylum in Russia, China, or South Africa</span>, then dropped the idea, and the father <span class="said">came back to Mexico City to run the protection himself</span>. What follows is a reading of that account: a story about the most powerful movement in Mexico discovering it cannot shield its own. Nearly all of it rests on one journalist's word. The marks say which parts.
</Lede>

<Section number="01" title="A <em>family matter</em> moves to Mexico City">

<Standfirst>The former president left Palenque for the capital, and the reason is not ceremonial. In this account, it is his son.</Standfirst>

Andrés Manuel López Obrador was in Palenque when the decision came. <span class="said">He returned to Mexico City to coordinate the protection of his son</span>, Andrés López Beltrán, against investigations being run from the United States. It is a father's move before it is a politician's.

Underneath everything that follows sits one premise: <span class="said">US intelligence and judicial agencies are actively building files against the López Obrador family and its associates, over alleged ties to organized crime</span>.

The account goes further than one return trip. <span class="said">The former president has moved his base of operations several times around the Mexico City area, to avoid capture by US agencies</span>. Which raises the question the next section takes up: why come back at all, when the family had been discussing the opposite?

</Section>

<Section number="02" title="The flight that was <em>weighed and dropped</em>">

<Standfirst>Before the return came the debate about leaving. Three countries were named, and then the idea was dropped for reasons that were political to the bone.</Standfirst>

Before the return, there was the question of exit. <span class="said">Inside the family, asylum was discussed seriously, and three countries were named: Russia, China, South Africa</span>. No document confirms the conversation, which happened behind closed doors. The list alone suggests how far the family thought the American reach might extend, and where it might stop.

Then <span class="said">the plan was dropped</span>, and the stated logic is the tell. Leaving would read as guilt. A confession made in a departure lounge is a political death before any legal one, and a movement whose founder flees may not outlive the concession. So the family stayed, and staying has its own price.

<PullQuote>The deep weakness of not being able even to defend your own in Mexico.</PullQuote>

That sentence is the column's verdict, and it earns a second reading. The man who built Mexico's governing movement cannot, on this account, keep his own son out of an American file. The file, it turns out, has a general's name in it.

</Section>

<Section number="03" title="What the general <em>handed over</em>">

<Standfirst>One name runs through the son's letter and everything around it: a Mexican general who is talking to American prosecutors.</Standfirst>

Gerardo Mérida is a Mexican general, and <span class="attr">he is acting as a cooperating witness for the US Department of Justice</span>. A cooperating witness trades what he knows to prosecutors in exchange for consideration, which can mean leniency. <span class="attr">What he handed over concerns the relationship between Andrés López Beltrán and Los Chapitos</span>, a faction of the Sinaloa Cartel.

<ContextBox label="What are Los Chapitos?">
Los Chapitos is the name of one faction of the Sinaloa Cartel, the Mexican drug-trafficking organization. Its longtime leader, Ismael 'El Mayo' Zambada, was captured and is now in American hands. That capture hangs over everything that follows, a reminder that the files are real and the reach is long.
</ContextBox>

<QABlock speaker="Riva Palacio">
Mérida had handed over information about the alleged relationship between Andrés López Beltrán and Los Chapitos.
</QABlock>

Against that background, the visa. <span class="said">Andrés López Beltrán's US visa was cancelled about five months before he acknowledged it</span>, in a public letter dated August 13. The general's cooperation and the cancelled visa sit side by side, and <span class="said">one may explain the other</span>. Juxtaposition is not proof, but it empties rooms. The next section watches one empty.

</Section>

<Section number="04" title="The movement rallies, and <em>looks over its shoulder</em>">

<Standfirst>The letter forced the movement to answer. The answer came quickly, and under it ran a quieter count of who else the Americans might call.</Standfirst>

The letter was, on its face, a personal clarification. Inside the movement it landed as a summons, and <span class="said">Morena's apparatus moved to contain the fallout</span>. The founder's son had been touched by a foreign investigation, and every statement of support was also a measurement of risk.

<ContextBox label="What is Morena?">
Morena is the political party that grew out of Andrés Manuel López Obrador's movement and now governs Mexico. Its candidates win on the founder's name, and its machinery answers to his family in ways no statute describes. Its apparatus is the people who must defend the brand, because the brand is what they have.
</ContextBox>

Then there is a name that surfaces almost in passing. <span class="said">Amílcar Olán Aparicio is a business associate, and the worry is that the investigations widen to people like him</span>, with no biography and no defense attached. The anxiety itself is the fact: a movement counting its associates, wondering which of them interests an American prosecutor.

One figure is missing from the fallout, and it is the father. He does not appear in the damage control because he is busy elsewhere, doing what he has always done. That is the column's last movement, and its most revealing.

</Section>

<Section number="05" title="The father <em>keeps pushing</em>">

<Standfirst>This does not end with a family in hiding. It ends with a patriarch still working the phones, and with the risks that posture runs.</Standfirst>

Even now, <span class="said">López Obrador presses Rubén Rocha Moya</span>, the governor of Sinaloa. And <span class="said">he keeps working to influence the movement's choice of candidates</span>, the ordinary traffic of a Mexican power broker. Under American scrutiny, the ordinary becomes something else. Every favor is a data point; every meeting, a possible exhibit.

The tension goes unresolved. The weakness has changed nothing about the habits: the same hands stay on the same levers, while somewhere in the American system a cooperating witness is still talking, or has already finished. Which of those is true matters more than anything the family publishes.

What remains open is concrete. Whether a general's cooperation becomes an indictment carrying a surname the whole country knows. Whether a cancelled visa was housekeeping or the first page of a file. Whether the movement's defense of the son survives the day a docket goes public. And whether the next letter from the family is a defense, or the opening of a negotiation.

</Section>
